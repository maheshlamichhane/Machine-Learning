Clustering
-> Clustering is similar to classification,but the basis is different. In Clustering you don't know what you are
looking for, and you are trying to identify some segments or clusters in your data. When you use clustering
algorithms on your dataset, unexpected things can suddenly pop up like structures, clusters and groupings you
would have never thought of otherwise.
In this part, you will understand and learn how to implement the following Machine Learning Clustering models.
i. K-Means Clustering
ii. Hierarchical Clustering


In Machine Learning, clustering means grouping similar data points together based on their characteristics or
similarities.

Think of it as automatically organizing data into different groups without telling the machine in advance
which data belongs to which group.

Clustering is an example of Unsupervised Machine Learning.
1. Understand with a real-world example
Imagine you own a supermarket and have information about 1,000 customers.

You have two features:
- Annual income
- Spending score
You want to identify different types of customers.

However, you don't know which customer belongs to which category.

A clustering algorithm analyzes the data and automatically creates groups.

For example:
- Cluster 1: High income, high spending.
- Cluster 2: Low income, low spending.
- Cluster 3: High income, low spending.
The algorithm discovers these groups based on similarities in the data.

Notice something important: We never told the algorithm that there should be three groups.
It discovered the groups itself.

K-Means Clustering in Machine Learning
K-Means is an unsupervised machine learning algorithm that divides a dataset into K different clusters (groups) based on the similarity of data points.

In simple words, it groups similar data points together and separates dissimilar data points.

Let's understand it step by step.
1. Simple real-world example
Imagine you own a shopping mall and have 6 customers.

You have collected two pieces of information about each customer:
- Annual income (in thousands).
- Spending score (out of 100).
Your dataset looks like this:
Customer	Annual income ($K)	Spending score
A	20	80
B	25	85
C	22	78
D	80	20
E	85	25
F	78	18

Now, your objective is to divide these customers into 2 groups based on their income and spending behavior.

But you don't know which customer belongs to which group.

This is where K-Means clustering comes into play.
2. How does K-Means work?
K-Means follows these steps:

Step 1: Choose K (number of clusters)

First, decide how many clusters you want.

For our example:

K = 2

This means we want to divide our 6 customers into 2 groups.

Step 2: Initialize centroids

What is a centroid?

A centroid is the center point of a cluster.

Initially, the algorithm selects 2 random points as centroids.

For example:
- Centroid 1 = (20, 80)
- Centroid 2 = (80, 20)
These are our initial cluster centers.

Step 3: Calculate the distance

Now the algorithm calculates the distance between every customer and both centroids.

It generally uses Euclidean distance.

The formula is:
\[
d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}
\]

For example, consider customer B (25, 85).

Distance from centroid 1 (20, 80):
\[
d_1=\sqrt{(25-20)^2+(85-80)^2}
\]

\[
d_1=\sqrt{25+25}=7.07
\]

Distance from centroid 2 (80, 20):
\[
d_2=\sqrt{(25-80)^2+(85-20)^2}
\]

\[
d_2=\sqrt{3025+4225}=85.15
\]

Since 7.07 is smaller than 85.15, customer B belongs to Cluster 1.

The algorithm performs this calculation for all customers.

Step 4: Assign data points to clusters

After calculating distances, the algorithm assigns each customer to the nearest centroid.

Our result:
Cluster 1	Cluster 2
Customer A	Customer D
Customer B	Customer E
Customer C	Customer F

Now we have two groups of customers.

Step 5: Recalculate centroids

This is an important step.

The algorithm calculates the new centroid by taking the average of all data points belonging to that cluster.

For Cluster 1:
\[
C_1=\left(\frac{20+25+22}{3},\frac{80+85+78}{3}\right)
\]

\[
C_1=(22.33,81)
\]

For Cluster 2:
\[
C_2=\left(\frac{80+85+78}{3},\frac{20+25+18}{3}\right)
\]

\[
C_2=(81,21)
\]

These become the new centroids.

Step 6: Repeat the process

Now the algorithm:
1. Calculates distances using the new centroids.
2. Assigns customers to their nearest centroids.
3. Recalculates centroids.
4. Repeats the process until the clusters stop changing (or another stopping condition is reached).
Final result
We have successfully divided our customers into two clusters.
3. What is the main objective of K-Means?
The main objective is to minimize the distance between data points and their respective cluster centroids.

In other words:
- Data points within the same cluster should be close to each other.
- Different clusters should ideally be separated from each other.
This is achieved by minimizing the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS).
\[
WCSS=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{x\in C_i}
||x-\mu_i||^2
\]

Where:
- K = Number of clusters.
- \(C_i\) = Data points belonging to cluster i.
- \(\mu_i\) = Centroid of cluster i.
- x = Individual data point.
4. What if we don't know the number of clusters (K)?
Good question!

Sometimes we don't know whether our dataset should have 2, 3, 4, or 5 clusters.

For this, we commonly use the Elbow Method.

The Elbow Method helps us determine a suitable number of clusters by plotting:
- X-axis: Number of clusters (K).
- Y-axis: WCSS.
We select a K near the point where adding more clusters produces diminishing improvements in WCSS.

We will explore this when learning the Elbow Method.

5. Important things to remember
Concept	Meaning
K	Number of clusters
Centroid	Center point of a cluster
Euclidean distance	Distance between two points
WCSS	Measures within-cluster squared distances
Elbow Method	Helps select a suitable K
Iteration	Repeating assignment and centroid calculation

One important limitation: K-Means is sensitive to feature scales. For example, if income ranges from 20,000 to
100,000 but spending scores range from 0 to 100, income could dominate the distance calculation. Therefore,
feature scaling (such as StandardScaler) is generally important.

My recommendation: Understand these three concepts before moving to Python implementation:
1. How centroids are initialized.
2. How Euclidean distance determines cluster membership.
3. How centroids are recalculated until convergence.
Once you understand these, the K-Means Python implementation using KMeans from sklearn.cluster will be much easier.


Elbow Method in Machine Learning
The Elbow Method is a technique used in K-Means Clustering to find the optimal number of clusters (K) for a given dataset.

Remember, in K-Means, we must specify the number of clusters before training the model.

But how do we know whether our dataset should have 2, 3, 4, or 5 clusters?

This is exactly the problem the Elbow Method helps solve.
1. Understand with a simple example
Imagine you have 100 customers in a shopping mall.

You want to group them based on:
- Annual income.
- Spending score.
Initially, you don't know how many groups would be appropriate.

So, you try different values of K:
- K = 1
- K = 2
- K = 3
- K = 4
- K = 5
- K = 6
For each K, you train a K-Means model and calculate its WCSS.
2. What is WCSS?
WCSS stands for Within-Cluster Sum of Squares.

It measures how far the data points are from their respective cluster centroids.

In simple words:

It measures how tightly the data points are grouped around their cluster centers.

The formula is:
\[
WCSS=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{x\in C_i}
||x-\mu_i||^2
\]

Where:
- K = Number of clusters.
- \(C_i\) = Data points in cluster i.
- \(\mu_i\) = Centroid of cluster i.
- x = Individual data point.
Important: Lower WCSS means data points are closer to their respective centroids.








K-Means++ in Machine Learning
K-Means++ is an improved initialization technique for the K-Means clustering algorithm.

Its main purpose is to select better initial centroids so that K-Means can produce better clusters and converge more efficiently.

Let's understand why we need it.
1. What problem does K-Means++ solve?
Remember how K-Means works?

The first step is to select K random points as initial centroids.

For example, suppose we have 100 customer records and want to create 3 clusters.

K-Means randomly selects 3 points as centroids.

But there is a problem!

What if all 3 randomly selected centroids are very close to each other?

This can lead to poor initial clustering and potentially a poor final result.

K-Means++ solves this problem by selecting initial centroids that are spread out from each other.
2. How does K-Means++ work?
Let's understand its steps with a simple example.

Suppose we have 8 customers and want to create 3 clusters.

Step 1: Select the first centroid randomly.

K-Means++ randomly selects one data point from the dataset as the first centroid.

For example:

Centroid 1 = Customer A.

Step 2: Calculate the distance from the first centroid.

Now calculate the distance between every data point and the first centroid.

For example:
Customer	Distance from centroid 1
A	0
B	2
C	3
D	5
E	10
F	12
G	15
H	20

Step 3: Select the second centroid using probability.

Here is the important part.

K-Means++ gives a higher probability of selection to data points that are farther away from the existing centroids.

It uses the squared distance:
\[
P(x_i)=\frac{D(x_i)^2}{\sum_j D(x_j)^2}
\]

Where:
- \(D(x_i)\) = Distance of point \(x_i\) from its nearest existing centroid.
- The denominator = Sum of squared distances of all data points.
For example:

Customer H has a distance of 20, while customer B has a distance of 2.

Their squared distances are:
\[
H=20^2=400
\]

\[
B=2^2=4
\]

Therefore, customer H has a much higher probability of being selected as the second centroid.

Notice that it is probabilistic, not guaranteed. A farther point is more likely to be selected, but not certain.

Step 4: Select the third centroid.

Now we have two centroids.

For every data point, calculate its distance from the nearest of these two centroids.

Again, use squared distances to calculate selection probabilities.

A point far from both existing centroids has a higher probability of becoming the third centroid.

Step 5: Start normal K-Means.

Once all 3 initial centroids are selected:
1. Calculate distances between data points and centroids.
2. Assign points to their nearest centroids.
3. Recalculate centroid positions.
4. Repeat until convergence.
That's it!
3. Difference between K-Means and K-Means++
K-Means	K-Means++
Randomly initializes centroids	Carefully selects initial centroids
Initial centroids may be close together	Encourages centroids to be spread out
Can produce poor initial clusters	Usually produces better initialization
May converge to a poor local solution	Often improves the quality of the final solution

Important: K-Means++ does not guarantee the globally optimal clustering result. It improves the initialization process.
