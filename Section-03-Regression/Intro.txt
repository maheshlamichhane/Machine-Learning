Simple Linear Regression
y^ = b0 +b1X1
where
y^ = Dependent variable
b0 = y-intercept
b1 = slope coefficient
X1 = Independent variable


This is the most important concept in Linear Regression. Once you understand this, you will understand what actually happens inside regressor.fit() and regressor.predict().

Let's understand it mathematically, using a real example.
1. How does Machine Learning find the straight line?
Suppose you provide this training dataset:
Experience (X)	Salary (Y)
1	30,000
2	40,000
3	50,000
4	60,000
5	70,000

We want to find a straight line:
\[
y = mx+c
\]

But we don't know the values of m and c.

Machine Learning needs to calculate them.

The objective is to find the line that produces the smallest possible prediction errors across the training data.
2. How does it calculate m (slope) and c (intercept)?
Scikit-learn's LinearRegression uses a mathematical technique called Ordinary Least Squares.

It calculates the following:

Slope (m):
\[
m = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}
{\sum (x_i-\bar{x})^2}
\]

Intercept (c):
\[
c = \bar{y}-m\bar{x}
\]

Where:
- \(x_i\) = Individual experience value.
- \(y_i\) = Individual salary value.
- \(\bar{x}\) = Average experience.
- \(\bar{y}\) = Average salary.
Let's calculate them using our example.

Average experience:
\[
\bar{x} = \frac{1+2+3+4+5}{5}=3
\]

Average salary:
\[
\bar{y} = \frac{30000+40000+50000+60000+70000}{5}
=50000
\]

Substituting into the slope formula:
\[
m = \frac{100000}{10}=10000
\]

Now calculate the intercept:
\[
c = 50000-(10000\times3)
\]

\[
c=20000
\]

Calculated result
Our final equation is:
\[
\boxed{y=10000x+20000}
\]

This is the best-fit straight line for our training dataset.
3. What if the training data isn't perfectly aligned?
Consider a slightly different dataset:
Experience	Actual Salary
1	32,000
2	39,000
3	52,000
4	58,000
5	72,000

Now the data points don't form a perfect straight line.

Machine Learning calculates a line that minimizes the sum of squared differences between actual salaries and predicted salaries.

For each data point:
\[
Error = Actual\ Salary-Predicted\ Salary
\]

And:
\[
SSE = \sum Error^2
\]

Why square the errors?
- Negative errors become positive.
- Larger errors receive greater penalties.
- Errors cannot cancel each other out.
This is called the Least Squares Method.
4. How does it calculate test data?
Now comes the interesting part!

Suppose our model has already learned:
\[
y=10000x+20000
\]

We provide new test data:
Experience (X_test)
6
7
8

The model simply substitutes these values into the learned equation.

For X = 6:
\[
y=10000(6)+20000=80000
\]

For X = 7:
\[
y=10000(7)+20000=90000
\]

For X = 8:
\[
y=10000(8)+20000=100000
\]

Prediction results
Experience	Predicted salary
6 years	80,000
7 years	90,000
8 years	100,000
